# Personal data regimes compared — GDPR vs PIPL

> One question: **how does each side decide when personal data may be processed, and what happens when it goes wrong?**
> Short answer: **the skeleton is similar; the keystone is different.** The GDPR's flexible basis is legitimate interests; PIPL has no such basis, so consent carries far more weight — and China adds a second, stricter layer: separate consent.
>
> Evidence grades (defined in [`00-overview.md`](00-overview.md)): **A** primary text, checked live; **B** other official source or a direct reading of the primary text; **C** our own comparison or inference, or not yet checked.

## 2. Five observations

1. **Same skeleton, different keystone.** The GDPR's escape hatch for ordinary business processing is legitimate interests, subject to a balancing test and a right to object. PIPL has no equivalent, so where there is no contract or legal duty, **consent does the work**. — **C**
2. **Separate consent is a distinct device.** Under PIPL, the highest-risk acts need a second, specific consent on top of ordinary consent. The GDPR's nearest analogue is "explicit consent", and it is confined to special categories and similar cases. — **A**
3. **The penalty arithmetic differs.** The GDPR takes a slice of **worldwide** turnover; PIPL caps at a fixed RMB 50m or 5% of **prior-year** turnover, and adds non-monetary sanctions (suspension, licence revocation) plus personal liability for responsible individuals. — **A**
4. **Both reach offshore, differently.** The GDPR exports a rights-and-transfers model; PIPL exports a security-and-assessment model with three gateways and periodic relaxation of thresholds. — **C**
5. **Convergence is underrated.** Both require a named accountability figure and a documented impact assessment before high-risk processing — closer than the usual "EU strict, China different" framing suggests. — **C**

## 3. What this means in practice

- Processing in both markets: **one template will not do.** Legitimate interests will not carry you in China; separate consent has no GDPR equivalent to slot into. — **C**
- Cross-border flows: the EU trusts an adequacy finding or standard clauses; China requires an act per flow — assessment, contract or certification. — **B**
- Sensitive data: China's list is broader in an everyday sense (financial accounts, whereabouts) and includes all data of children under 14 by definition. — **A**
